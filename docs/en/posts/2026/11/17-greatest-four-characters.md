# What Are the Greatest Four Chinese Characters in the World?

<!-- DESC SEP -->

From “Long live the people” to “democracy and freedom,” Mao Buming looks at the individual people behind the slogans and their right to decide for themselves.

<!-- DESC SEP -->

Someone asked what the greatest four Chinese characters in the world were. Many of the answers said: 人民万岁. Long live the people.

I stared at those four characters for a while and felt something rather strange. It was like sitting an exam where the question asked whom you loved most, and several thousand candidates all lowered their heads to write the standard answer. Then the invigilator nodded approvingly, satisfied that this year's candidates were emotionally sound.

That is not to say they were lying. Of course the people can live long. Emperors have been wished ten thousand years of life for thousands of years. It ought to be the people's turn by now. Besides, there is usually only one emperor, while there are a great many people. Going by the numbers, they ought to outlive him by quite a stretch.

But I still wanted to know who the people actually were.

The woman pushing her cart to the market at four in the morning, or the man shuttling from office to office with a stack of documents after breaking his leg on a construction site? The child in a county town whose family cannot afford extra tutoring, or the old person checking their bank balance over and over at the hospital payment window?

Of course all of them are the people. It is just that, while they are alive, nobody usually rushes over to wish them ten thousand years.

If the vegetable seller moves her stall half a meter farther out, someone tells her not to spoil the city's appearance. If a worker's wages are months overdue, someone tells him to appreciate the company's difficulties. If a child fails to get into a good school, relatives say he did not work hard enough. If an old person cannot afford treatment, someone nearby explains that birth, aging, illness, and death are all part of life.

The moment a person becomes someone specific, all sorts of faults appear. He takes up space, asks for wages, cries out in pain, complains. Just as everyone is getting ready to sing the praises of life, he pulls a crumpled bill out of his pocket.

Everyone has just polished the word “people” until it gleams, and in he walks with mud on his shoes, saying he has not paid this month's electricity bill.

How inconsiderate.

So the people had better stay inside the slogans. There is plenty of room in there, no need to eat, and no social insurance contributions to pay. They are orderly, hardworking, and kind, with simple smiles permanently on their faces. Suffering makes them resilient, poverty makes them noble, and their silence proves that they put the greater good first.

As for some particular person who suddenly no longer wants to be resilient, who wants a little more pay, a little less suffering, a chance to sit down and rest, it becomes hard to say whether he still counts as the people. Perhaps he is just selfish.

I eventually noticed that many people love the people but do not much like people.

The people are grand. People are full of petty concerns. The people can go into a speech; a person will ask where the toilet is halfway through it. The people are willing to sacrifice for tomorrow; a person only knows that the landlord is chasing today's rent again. The people can make history; a person gets home from work and wants to lie down, barely willing to take a shower.

Praise the people and he will applaud. Ask him to listen to a stranger talk about their grievances for half an hour, and he may take out his phone and scroll through a couple of videos.

There is nothing so terrible about that. Nobody is obliged to like everyone. I do not either. When someone on the bus plays short videos aloud, my love for the people generally lasts only until the next stop. When someone jumps the queue, I do not think of him as a maker of history. I only wonder why history could not have given him some manners while it was at it.

That is how people are. A person can be great and petty; he can leave the last half bowl of rice for his child and argue with a street vendor for ages over two yuan.

The people have never been as tidy as they look in propaganda posters. They sweat, lie, hunt for bargains, and, after being bullied, bully someone weaker.

But these are the people.

They do not earn the name by being sufficiently noble. They are alive. They have stomachs, desires, private motives they are ashamed to admit, and a whole heap of wishes they have not yet fulfilled. That already makes them the people.

People like these, however, are inconvenient to praise. A hungry person will ask where the food is. A sick person will ask why medicine costs so much. A person whose wages have been withheld will not be satisfied to hear how great he is. He holds out his hand to get back the money that belongs to him, not to receive a flag.

Once that hand reaches out, the occasion loses its solemnity.

So often, the strangest thing about “Long live the people” is neither “the people” nor “long live.” It is where the person saying it is standing.

If someone standing in a cornfield says it to another person drenched in sweat, I think that is fine.

If someone sitting in a rented room has just received six months of overdue wages, drinks two beers, and suddenly shouts it out the window, I think that is fine too.

Even if a child comes home from school to find that his father has not been drinking today, his mother has not been crying, and the family finishes a quiet meal together, those four characters carry some weight when he whispers them to himself.

But if someone stands high above everyone else, lights shining on his face, a dark mass of people seated below, and spreads his arms to proclaim “Long live the people,” I always want to see what happens once it is over.

What time do the people putting away the chairs get off work?

Do the cleaners get overtime pay?

Has water already seeped into the shoes of the person keeping order at the door?

If nobody cares about these things, perhaps the people onstage have already been granted their ten thousand years, while the people below still have another night to get through.

I once helped an old man harvest corn. The sun was fierce, and the leaves left little cuts all over my arms. His birthday was coming up, and he asked me, as someone who had been to school, to say something nice.

I thought for a long time and said, “Long live the people.”

He stared for a moment, called me a lunatic, then doubled over laughing for a long time.

I laughed too.

That day there was no podium, no applause, and nobody standing by to judge whether those four characters were correct enough. There were just two people sweating under the sun in a disorderly patch of corn.

It suddenly occurred to me that perhaps this was how “Long live the people” ought to be said.

Let one particular person hear it.

Then let him laugh.

After I wrote about “Long live the people,” someone said the greatest four characters in the world ought to be 民主自由: democracy and freedom.

I looked at that for a while and still felt something was off.

It was rather like a father calling his child to the dinner table and solemnly announcing that this family did not practice feudal despotism: your life is yours to choose.

The child thinks for a moment and makes a choice. The father falls silent for a few seconds, then begins to explain why the choice is immature, why it is impractical, why it lets his parents down, and why he will certainly regret it.

By midnight, after all this analysis, the child has finally regained his freedom.

He freely chooses what his father has chosen for him.

Democracy and freedom are, of course, good things. The trouble is that once people say those four characters aloud, they tend to quietly attach a few conditions of use.

You may speak, but you had better be rational.

You may choose, but you had better not choose wrong.

You may object, but do not get in the way of the bigger picture.

You may be yourself, but that self had better be respectable, clearheaded, and progressive, someone who does not irritate everyone else.

Otherwise people will start to wonder whether you have learned how to be free.

This is rather interesting. The person saying “Long live the people” tends to stand above the people. The person saying “democracy and freedom” prefers to sit in the examiner's chair. You exercise your freedom; he decides whether your freedom passes muster. The people make their choice; he marks their answers.

If the answer pleases him, the people have awakened. If it does not, he begins to say that the people are ignorant, poorly educated, easily manipulated, and not yet capable of choosing correctly.

And so the people have gained the right to choose, but they can never choose right.

Democracy becomes a very large exam. Everyone may answer the questions, but someone has already written the answer key. If you insist on writing something else, the invigilator will sigh over your paper and say that this year's people are still not up to standard.

But democracy was never a competition to pick out the cleverest people.

If you must be knowledgeable, emotionally stable, logically rigorous, and have read all the material before you deserve a ballot, the vast majority of the world's people will first have to be expelled.

Things become convenient for the few who remain. They are both the people and the experts. They make the choices and declare that their choices represent the people. In the end, a handful of people sit in a room applauding one another, practicing democracy in such peace and quiet that there is not even an argument.

Actual democracy seldom looks so good.

Individual people can be stubborn, petty, and emotional in their decisions. Someone believes something stupid today and changes his mind tomorrow. He does not understand your magnificent plan; he only cares when the road outside his home will be repaired. He may even use the rights you have handed him to choose an outcome you thoroughly dislike.

Whether you can still recognize him as one of the people at that point is where the question really begins.

When people talk about democracy, they tend to imagine themselves as the person who finally gets the microphone. They seldom imagine that, when the microphone passes to someone else, that person might call them an idiot in public.

When people talk about freedom, they also picture themselves breaking free of the ropes. A father thinks of his child finally escaping poverty. He rarely considers that one day the person his child wants to escape might be him.

When a boss likes employees who think independently, he probably hopes they will independently come up with ways to make the company money, rather than independently wonder in a meeting why the boss gets the largest share.

When a man says he likes independent women, he may not be praising their ability to leave whenever they want.

The freedom many people like is the freedom of others to come toward them without restraint. If those others turn around and head somewhere entirely different, that freedom begins to look rather inconsiderate.

Because the real trouble with freedom has never been whether someone can make admirable choices.

It is that someone has the right to make a mess of their life.

They can study a subject with little practical use, turn down what everyone else considers a good job, love the wrong person, or, after someone has planned everything for them, say, “I don't want it.”

They may not even have a particularly sophisticated reason to give afterward.

They just do not want it.

Nothing is more infuriating.

We are used to calculating other people's prospects, weighing their options, and arranging their happiness. We are willing to acknowledge that a person is free in principle, but find it hard to accept when they actually refuse to do as they are told.

So freedom, too, becomes a prize. Rational people may collect it. Mature people may collect it. People who can take responsibility for their choices may collect it.

As for someone who is being foolish, hesitating, or still has no idea what to do with their life, everyone decides to hold it for safekeeping. Once they learn to use freedom correctly, they can have it back.

It sounds very considerate.

Except that one person's correctness is still decided by someone else. The key is in that other person's hand. The door can be opened at any time, provided you promise to follow their route once you get outside.

So my strange feeling about “democracy and freedom” does not come from anything wrong with those four characters. What is strange is that the people saying them often only want democracy for themselves and freedom for their own use.

They want their voices heard but may not be willing to hear voices that are grating, vulgar, or even opposed to them. They demand respect for their choices, but when someone else chooses to leave them, they decide that person has been misled.

In the end, what people like most has never been freedom.

They like being in charge of their own affairs.

As for other people wanting to be in charge too, that will require another meeting.

I once watched a family discuss what their child should study at university. The father said many times that the family was very democratic. Whatever the final choice, it would be entirely up to the child.

The child quietly named a subject. His father's face immediately darkened.

For the next hour or more, everyone took turns trying to persuade him. His mother talked about employment, relatives talked about his future, and his father talked about how hard it had been to put him through school all these years.

The child sat through it, lowered his head, and changed his application.

Over dinner, the father said to me with some satisfaction, “See? We didn't force him. He came around on his own.”

I nodded.

The child did indeed have freedom.

The whole family had simply helped him use it in the right place.
