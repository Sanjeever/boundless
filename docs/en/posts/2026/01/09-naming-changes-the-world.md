# Naming

<!-- DESC SEP -->

From variables and relationships to workplace jargon, names help us point to things quickly while quietly sparing us the trouble of asking what they contain.

<!-- DESC SEP -->

## Only Two Hard Things

There is an old joke in computer science: there are only two hard things in programming—cache invalidation and naming things.

`a`, `b`, `data`, `temp`, or `flag1` and `flag2` when patience runs out. Giving a machine distinct identifiers is trivial; the hard part is letting the next person who opens the file see what a thing represents and where its responsibilities end.

`isValid` promises a boolean. Once the business also needs “validating” and “partially valid,” renaming the variable will not save a muddled state model. And if a class called `LoginController` quietly initializes database connections and sweeps up old logs on the side, the program will still run, but the reader has been handed a faulty map.

Outside of code, the same sleight of hand happens all the time. A name begins as a doorway into understanding, yet we easily mistake knowing what something is called for knowing what it is.

## Scripting Relationships

Nowhere are we more eager to name things than in human relationships.

When two people grow close, admiration, reliance, attraction, intellectual kinship, and a trace of rivalry or hesitation often arrive tangled together. Those feelings rarely surface on schedule, and they rarely fit neatly inside a single ready-made word.

Yet the moment someone asks, “What are you two?”, whatever is still taking shape between them has to find a box to live in.

Friends? Lovers? Something else?

Picking a title brings relief and a sense of footing, but the title often smuggles in a script neither person wrote. Daily good-night messages, holiday gifts, and unspoken rules of exclusivity turn from shared impulses into obligations. A single question—“Isn’t that what lovers do?”—can shut down a conversation that needed to happen.

The quiet rhythm that belonged only to two specific people gets overwritten by a generic class. The argument drifts from what each person actually needs to whether someone is playing the role properly.

## Labels for Moods

Turned inward, a name can act like a convenient seal.

Someone feels hollow in the late afternoon, tears up watching the light fade, finds work pointless, and wants to board a train going anywhere far away.

Today a handful of words are always within reach: depression, anxiety, burnout.

Medical and psychological terms help people find real care when they need it, and that matters deeply. In everyday talk, though, a label often lands so fast that the inquiry stops right there, as if naming the fog explained where it came from or what kind of exhaustion sits underneath.

Casual tags like “social anxiety” or “procrastination” work the same way. The word snaps shut like a standard-issue box, hiding the uneven shape of the trouble inside.

## Workplace Rhetoric

At work, changing a name is often a way of moving the real cost out of sight.

Programmers know the word “sprint” well. In the [2020 Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#the-sprint), a Sprint is simply a fixed window of a month or less, meant to sustain a steady rhythm. Borrowed into everyday speech, though, “sprinting” means running flat out until your lungs burn. String one sprint directly after another, and an emergency dash quietly becomes the baseline pace.

Another favorite is “optimization.”

When a company lays people off after bad bets or a cash crunch, the memo calls it an “organizational optimization.” Firing sounds heavy; it carries mortgages, cardboard boxes, and long silences at the dinner table. Optimization sounds clean and mathematical, like trimming a few dead lines of code.

The person signing the sheet handles numbers in a spreadsheet; the person leaving calculates next month’s rent at midnight. The distance between the two words is the exact distance between making a decision and living with it.

## What a Name Leaves Out

In software, we cannot live without abstraction. When we call `sort()`, we rarely care which algorithm runs underneath—until the day our data needs a stable sort, and we have to open the documentation to read the fine print.

Names in daily life are abstractions of the same kind. They spare us the exhaustion of explaining everything from scratch, and they always leave things out.

Using a name for its speed, while pausing when it matters to ask what was left on the cutting-room floor, is how we keep our own words from thinking for us.
