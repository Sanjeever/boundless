# Teenage Playlists and Adult Appetites

<!-- DESC SEP -->

Starting from a New York Times Spotify study and a VICE interview, a look at my middle-school record wall and the deeper aesthetic baseline that outlasts any playlist.

<!-- DESC SEP -->

Not long ago I stumbled across two pieces from 2018: a VICE interview titled [We Asked an Expert Why We Stay Obsessed with Music From Our Teens](https://www.vice.com/en/article/we-asked-an-expert-why-we-stay-obsessed-with-music-from-our-teens/), and the _New York Times_ column it cites, [The Songs That Bind](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/02/10/opinion/sunday/favorite-songs.html).

On the Chinese internet, those two articles often circulate as a single, fatalistic aphorism: your musical taste locks in during adolescence, making it almost impossible to love anything different after 25—and the same goes for everything else you care about as an adult.

Reading that line made me click open my own [records wall](/en/albums).

## The dividing line on the wall

Every album on my [records wall](/en/albums) released in 2020 or earlier comes straight from what I had on repeat in middle school and high school.

Those playlists wandered all over the map: [Pink Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_Floyd), [King Crimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Crimson), and [Radiohead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiohead); instrumental post-rock like [Doomina](https://doomina.bandcamp.com/album/beauty); [Frank Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Ocean), [Joji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joji), and [Dean Ting](https://music.apple.com/tw/album/1876189253); alongside Mandarin records from Cheer Chen, Deserts Chang, LaLa Hsu, and Sandy Lam's _Gaia_ and _0_.

Placed side by side, though, those wildly different covers betray a single listening habit. Whether at fifteen or twenty-two, I barely paid attention to lyrics.

My ear always went straight to the composition itself—how the chords resolved, how the rhythm section locked into a groove, how the instruments were layered, and the mood carried directly by texture and tone.

That habit left me permanently immune to formulaic pop ballads. Whenever a song treats its arrangement like a photocopied template and leans on literal lyrics to instruct the listener when to feel heartbroken, my attention quietly checks out.

After 2020, I kept hanging new sleeves on the wall—Liquify's instrumental psychedelic rock, Dean Ting's 2024 album _Back of Me_, and recent releases from Hsien-Ching Chen, Shan Yichun, and AG Club. The release years keep moving forward, yet the ruler I use to measure them is unmistakably the one from middle school.

## What the original articles actually said

Before accepting "everything freezes at 25" as a law of nature, I went back and read the two English pieces word for word. Two crucial nuances had been sanded off in the viral summary.

Start with the _New York Times_ numbers. Economist [Seth Stephens-Davidowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Stephens-Davidowitz) looked at Spotify streams across age groups for _Billboard_ hits released between 1960 and 2000. He found that a song's lasting grip on adults tracks how old they were the year it came out.

Radiohead's 1993 single "Creep," for instance, was the 164th most-streamed track among 38-year-old men in 2018—men who were 14 in 1993. For men born ten years earlier or ten years later, the song did not even crack the top 300.

Across all tracks, the main window for forming musical taste ran from ages 13 to 16 for men (peaking at 14) and 11 to 14 for women (peaking at 13). Both groups also showed a smaller secondary bump in their early twenties, roughly half as strong as the teenage peak.

Where, then, did the famous "after 25" deadline come from? Nowhere in the data.

It came from VICE writer Daisy Jones's opening joke about herself. She happened to be 25 when she wrote the article, felt more open to life than she had at 15, and wondered why her brain felt "cemented shut" around new music. A reporter's casual mention of her own age got telephone-gamed into a universal biological cutoff.

The second leap was "the same goes for everything else."

To find out why teen music sticks so hard, Jones interviewed Dr. Stephanie Burnett Heyes, a psychology lecturer at the University of Birmingham. Burnett Heyes pointed to two forces: adolescence is a "social sensitive" period when we are unusually receptive to peers and new ideas, and the teenage brain fires more intensely in response to rewarding stimuli than it does in childhood or later adulthood.

Right at the end of the interview, Jones asked whether other tastes—like film or fashion—lock in during our teens too.

Burnett Heyes immediately narrowed the claim. Music is a classic case, she said, whereas "the others aren't as clear cut"—teenagers rarely have the money to buy the clothes they actually want.

Instead, she named a different realm that genuinely tends to crystallize in youth: spiritual beliefs, attitudes, and ideological preferences.

## Lightness, exactitude, and concrete things

Compared with the vague claim that "everything you like is decided in high school," Burnett Heyes's pairing of music with deeper attitudes hits much closer to home.

When I take stock of what has stayed with me into adulthood, right beside listening for composition over lyrics is a very specific appetite for how ideas and sentences are put together.

I have no patience for grand narratives. Oversized slogans and floating abstractions leave me cold; I care about concrete things—a real person, a well-crafted tool, a mechanism whose cause and effect you can trace with your fingers. I cannot stay long in dull, airless rooms, and what draws me in is almost always intelligence and wit.

In fiction, I keep returning to [Italo Calvino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italo_Calvino). His pages have a weightless, unforced clarity—never stiff, never puffed up, leaving plenty of white space around each thought.

In the opening lecture of [Six Memos for the Next Millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Memos_for_the_Next_Millennium), Calvino spoke of "Lightness"—not vague drifting, but lifting the dead weight out of language through crisp imagery and exact structure—and paired it a few chapters later with "Exactitude."

That sensibility is what I think of as a scientific aesthetic: clean proportions, restraint, and beauty that rises naturally out of structure and logic rather than decorative padding. On my [About page](/en/about), I mentioned loving [Wang Xiaobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Xiaobo); what pulls me to his essays is the same candor, playfulness, and refusal to sound pompous.

Looked at side by side, all of these preferences grow from a single root.

A formulaic pop ballad grates because it skimps on musical structure and expects literal words about heartbreak to do the heavy lifting. A grand narrative grates for the same reason: it skips over concrete facts and real people, using loud nouns to demand applause.

Conversely, a well-voiced instrumental passage, a Calvino story, and a clean piece of code work on the mind in the exact same way. None of them raise their voice; they stand on proportion, breathing room, and quiet precision.

Once your brain tastes the intense reward of structural clarity and unforced emotion during those formative years, that scale becomes your default setting. Run into padded jargon or assembly-line sentimentality later in life, and the ear rejects it on instinct.

## Keeping the sieve, changing the room

Seen that way, an early baseline is hardly a life sentence.

Burnett Heyes left a telling clue at the end of her interview when she talked about that secondary spike in the early twenties. Why does taste open up again around then? Because people leave home, move to unfamiliar cities, walk into new rooms, and bump into people and ideas they have never met before.

When adults stop warming to anything new, it is rarely because their synapses have turned to concrete. More often, daily life has simply settled into a closed loop where nothing in the room asks a fresh question.

As for the baseline left over from my teens—listening for the pulse inside the music, loving Calvino's lightness, favoring a clean scientific aesthetic, trusting concrete things over grand narratives—it acts less like a wall and more like a sieve against noise.

That baseline decides what kind of craft and texture can make my ears perk up. So long as I keep walking out of dull rooms, the same sieve can keep catching unfamiliar things worth loving at thirty, forty, and beyond.
