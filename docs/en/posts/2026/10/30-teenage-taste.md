# Teenage Playlists and Adult Appetites

<!-- DESC SEP -->

A popular summary claims that musical taste locks in during adolescence, making it hard to love anything different after 25—and that the rest of our adult preferences are decided in our teens as well. Checking my own records wall and reading the two original articles points to something more specific.

<!-- DESC SEP -->

I recently came across two pieces from 2018: a VICE interview titled [We Asked an Expert Why We Stay Obsessed with Music From Our Teens](https://www.vice.com/en/article/we-asked-an-expert-why-we-stay-obsessed-with-music-from-our-teens/), and the _New York Times_ column it cites, [The Songs That Bind](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/02/10/opinion/sunday/favorite-songs.html).

A widely shared Chinese summary distills them into a neat aphorism: a person's musical taste is largely formed in adolescence, making it hard to fall for anything different after 25, and the same holds true elsewhere—what you love as an adult is mostly decided in your teenage years.

It is a striking claim, and it made me open my own [records wall](/en/albums).

## The Dividing Line on the Record Wall

Every album on my [records wall](/en/albums) released in 2020 or earlier comes from what I listened to and loved in middle school and high school.

Those playlists covered a lot of ground: [Pink Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_Floyd), [King Crimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Crimson), and [Radiohead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiohead); instrumental post-rock like [Doomina](https://doomina.bandcamp.com/album/beauty); [Frank Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Ocean), [Joji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joji), and [Dean Ting](https://music.apple.com/tw/album/1876189253); alongside Mandarin singer-songwriters and art-pop records from Cheer Chen, Deserts Chang, LaLa Hsu, and Sandy Lam's _Gaia_ and _0_.

Set side by side, those very different records point to one consistent listening habit. Whether in my teens or my early twenties, I barely paid attention to lyrics. My ear goes straight to the music itself—the chord voicings, rhythmic groove, instrumental arrangement, and the emotion carried directly by the sound.

Because of that habit, formulaic Mandarin pop ballads have never worked on me. When an arrangement treats the backing track as an assembly-line template and relies on literal lyrics to instruct the listener when to feel heartbroken, my attention shuts off.

After 2020, I kept adding new releases to the wall—Liquify's instrumental psychedelic rock, Dean Ting's 2024 album _Back of Me_, and recent records by Hsien-Ching Chen, Shan Yichun, and AG Club. The release dates move forward, yet the yardstick I use to pick them is still the one from middle and high school. In that sense, the musical appetite formed in my teens has stayed right where it was.

## What the Original Articles Actually Say

Before turning that personal observation into a rule that human taste freezes at 25, it is worth checking what the two English articles actually wrote. Reading the originals shows that two important conditions disappeared in the viral summary.

Start with the _New York Times_ data. Economist [Seth Stephens-Davidowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Stephens-Davidowitz) analyzed Spotify play counts across age cohorts for _Billboard_ hits released between 1960 and 2000. He found that a song's popularity among adults tracks how old listeners were when the track came out. Radiohead's 1993 single "Creep," for example, ranked as the 164th most popular song among 38-year-old men in 2018—men who were 14 in 1993—while failing to crack the top 300 for men born a decade earlier or a decade later.

Across the full dataset, the primary window for forming adult musical taste was ages 13 to 16 for men (peaking at 14) and ages 11 to 14 for women (peaking at 13). For both men and women, the early twenties formed a secondary window with roughly half the influence of early adolescence.

Where did the "after 25" cutoff come from? It does not appear in the _New York Times_ study at all. It comes from VICE writer Daisy Jones's opening paragraph. She happened to be 25 when writing the piece, felt open to new experiences around her, and wondered why her brain seemed "cemented shut" when it came to music. A journalist's self-deprecating remark about her own age got flattened into a universal biological deadline.

The bigger leap lies in "the same holds true for everything else."

To understand why early teen music sticks so stubbornly, Jones interviewed Dr. Stephanie Burnett Heyes, a psychology lecturer at the University of Birmingham. Burnett Heyes pointed to two mechanisms under study in developmental psychology. First, adolescence is a "social sensitive" period when people are unusually receptive to peers and new ideas, making interactions from those years more likely to endure. Second, functional brain activity in adolescents responds more intensely to stimulating rewards than in younger children or older adults. Because girls generally enter puberty a year or two earlier than boys, pubertal hormones affecting the brain also offer a straightforward hypothesis for the gender gap in the data. As for the smaller spike in the early twenties, she noted that researchers would need more data to tell whether it reflects another brain mechanism or social shifts like leaving home, going to university, and meeting new people.

At the end of the interview, Jones asked the exact question behind the viral summary: are our tastes formed in our teen years in other ways too, such as films and fashion?

Burnett Heyes pushed back against a blanket generalization. Music, she said, is a paradigmatic case, whereas "the others aren't as clear cut." Fashion is expensive for a teenager, so financial constraints get in the way. Instead, she pointed to a different domain that plausibly takes shape in adolescence: spiritual beliefs, attitudes, and ideological preferences, since teenagers tend to be more open to ideas about how the world works.

## Lightness, a Scientific Aesthetic, and Concrete Things

Compared with the vague claim that "everything you like as an adult is decided in your teens," Burnett Heyes's pairing of music with deeper attitudes and intellectual preferences matches my own experience much more closely.

Looking at what has stayed with me into adulthood, alongside listening for composition over lyrics, the clearest thread is how I relate to ideas, writing, and the environment around me.

I have no appetite for grand narratives. Compared with sweeping abstractions and hollow slogans, I care about concrete things: a real person, a well-made tool, a mechanism whose cause and effect can be traced step by step. I cannot stand staying in dull, lifeless environments for long, and what draws me in is intelligence and wisdom.

In fiction, I love [Italo Calvino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italo_Calvino) for how effortlessly light his pages feel. In [Six Memos for the Next Millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Memos_for_the_Next_Millennium), Calvino devoted his opening lecture to "Lightness"—removing weight from language through precise structure and crisp imagery—and followed it closely with "Exactitude." That sensibility aligns with what I think of as a scientific aesthetic: clean proportions, restraint, and beauty that emerges from structure and logic rather than ornamental padding. On my [About page](/en/about), I mentioned admiring [Wang Xiaobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Xiaobo); what attracts me in his essays is the same candor, refusal of boredom, and pursuit of wisdom.

Looked at together, these preferences grow out of the same root.

A formulaic pop ballad grates on the ear because it skimps on harmonic and rhythmic craft, relying on literal lyrics to tell the listener how to feel. A grand narrative grates in a similar way: it skips over concrete individuals and real constraints, using oversized nouns to demand emotional agreement. Conversely, an intricate instrumental passage, a Calvino story, and a clean piece of code work on the mind in much the same way. They dispense with loud rhetoric and stand on clear structure, exact proportion, and restraint.

Once your mind experiences the intense reward of structural clarity, genuine emotion, and intellectual play during that adolescent "social sensitive" window, that scale becomes your default setting. Meeting padded jargon, joyless preaching, or assembly-line sentimentality in adulthood naturally triggers resistance.

## Keeping the Baseline, Changing the Room

Seen this way, a baseline formed in youth need not be a prison sentence.

If "struggling to like different things after 25" meant closing yourself off to unfamiliar fields, tools, or perspectives, that would be a real loss. Yet Burnett Heyes's remark about the early-twenties spike offers a useful clue: tastes open up again when people leave home, enter a new place, and find themselves surrounded by different people and ideas.

When adults stop warming to anything new, the cause is often less a brain sealed in concrete than a life that has settled into a fixed track, where nothing in the environment demands fresh curiosity.

As for the baseline left over from my teens—listening for the emotion inside the composition, loving Calvino's lightness, favoring a clean scientific aesthetic, caring about concrete things, and steering clear of boredom and grand narratives—it works more like a filter against noise. That baseline decides what kind of craft and texture can move me. As long as I refuse to stay in dull environments, the same filter can keep turning up unfamiliar things worth loving in my thirties, forties, and beyond.
